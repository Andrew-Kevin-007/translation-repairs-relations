\title{Where Translation Helps and Where It Hurts: A Pre-Registered
Component-Level Study of Knowledge Graph Population from Indic-Language Text}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\institute{Anonymous Institution}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Populating knowledge bases such as Wikidata from text in low-resource languages
is limited by an unmeasured distinction: whether failures come from models not
understanding the source text, or from models failing to ground what they
understood into canonical knowledge-base identifiers. We test the hypothesis
that translating the input to English repairs relation identification but not
entity grounding\footnote{Per the gap-recovery criterion pre-registered in
Section~\ref{sec:instrument}.}, using two open 8B-class extraction systems across
eight Indic languages in two arms (direct extraction from the source
language, and translate-then-extract through English), with component-level
scoring that separates the relation and entity contributions to every error.
To make this measurable, we adapt the manually annotated portion of an existing
fact-to-text alignment resource~\cite{abhishek2022xalign} into a text-to-triple
extraction benchmark by deterministically re-grounding its English fact labels
in Wikidata, and we validate the adaptation with a native-speaker audit and
automatic cross-checking against the live knowledge base, quantifying the
adapted gold's miss rate. Under the frozen decision table the verdict lands in
the pre-registered mixed branch: translation improves relation-F1 in every
language for one system and seven of eight for the other (unclipped gap
recovery $+0.67$ to $+1.85$ over the five languages with a measurable
baseline gap; exact one-sided sign test $p=.031$, not surviving the
conservative family correction), while entity grounding presents no
verdict-eligible gap to repair under the frozen rule: the English control
is itself near-floor. For the system driving the paper's usable relation-repair
contrasts, translation reduces entity-F1 in seven of eight languages; our
archived-candidate decomposition attributes this to retrieval rather than
selection for that system, where models choose the gold entity in
92.9--99.5\% of calls whenever retrieval surfaces it (76.2--94.7\%
for the other system, whose retrieval pattern does not replicate the
direction). The automated validation bound is consistent with the
pre-registered reliability claim for gold reuse, pending confirmation by the
native-speaker audit that the frozen rule keys the claim to: across all
cells, at most 4.8\% (pooled 1.3\%) of predictions absent from the adapted
gold are true in the live knowledge base. We release the benchmark,
audit protocol, and evaluation harness, constructed so that further languages
can be added without bespoke annotation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: The Hypothesis and Why It Matters}\label{sec:intro}
% First paragraph = the hypothesis, verbatim from the pre-registration prose form.
% No pipeline advertising. Banned vocabulary list: PREREGISTRATION.md section 9.

This paper tests one pre-registered hypothesis: \emph{the low-resource penalty
in populating Wikidata from Indic-language text lies in entity
canonicalization, not relation understanding}: translating the input to
English repairs relation identification but not entity grounding. (We use
\emph{entity canonicalization} and \emph{entity grounding} interchangeably for
this second task: resolving an extracted mention to its Wikidata identifier.)
The hypothesis, its decision thresholds, and the mapping from outcomes to claims
were frozen in a public pre-registration before any model output was scored
(Section~\ref{sec:instrument}).

Why this distinction deserves a paper: the two failure modes call for opposite
remedies. Knowledge-graph population from text is, at minimum, two coupled
tasks: recognizing that a sentence asserts a relation, and grounding the
participants of that relation into canonical identifiers (Wikidata QIDs) that
the knowledge base can actually store. If low-resource failures are failures of
\emph{understanding}, then the remedy is better multilingual pretraining or
machine translation at inference time, and the situation improves as MT
improves. If they are failures of \emph{canonicalization}, translation cannot
help: the model may understand a sentence perfectly in translation and still be
unable to name the QID of an entity it has only ever seen written in another
script. These remedies are funded, engineered, and benchmarked by different
communities. Yet standard evaluation practice scores extracted triples
monolithically, so a missed triple does not say which community's problem it
was. The experiments here are designed so that every scored error is
attributable to one component or the other.

The languages under study (Hindi, Bengali, Telugu, Tamil, Kannada,
Malayalam, Marathi, and Gujarati, with an English control slice) are spoken
by over a billion people while remaining, by knowledge-base coverage and NLP
resource standards, low-resource. For these communities the question is not
academic: knowledge-base coverage of locally prominent entities shapes what
downstream systems (search, question answering, fact verification) can
know at all.

Concretely, we run two open multilingual LLMs (Qwen3-8B and
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct; Section~\ref{sec:test}) over the same evaluation items
in two arms: \textsc{direct}
(extraction from original-language text) and \textsc{pivot} (machine
translation to English~\cite{gala2023indictrans2}, then the identical English
extraction pipeline), against an \textsc{english} control ceiling. Scoring separates
relation-F1 from entity-F1, and the pre-registered \emph{gap recovery
ratio} (GRR; Section~\ref{sec:test}) measures, per component, how much of the
low-resource-to-English gap the translation step closes. A decision table
frozen before data maps every possible outcome pattern (including the ones
that falsify the hypothesis) to a designated claim, so the paper's headline
was determined by the results rather than selected after them.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
(1)~A text-to-triple extraction benchmark for eight Indic languages plus an
English control, constructed by reversing the manually annotated test split of
XAlign~\cite{abhishek2022xalign} and deterministically re-grounding its
English fact labels into Wikidata identifiers, with the adaptation's coverage
and drop reasons reported per language (Section~\ref{sec:instrument}).
(2)~A validation of that benchmark as an instrument: a native-speaker audit of
one full language subset plus automated live-Wikidata cross-checking of every
scored prediction, yielding a quantified estimate of gold miss rate
(Section~\ref{sec:instrument}).
(3)~A pre-registered, component-separated measurement of what translation does
and does not repair in low-resource KG population, with hierarchical-bootstrap
uncertainty and a frozen decision table (Sections~\ref{sec:test}
and~\ref{sec:secondary}). The measured outcome is the mixed branch:
translation repairs relation identification, while entity grounding
presents no verdict-eligible gap to repair and is, descriptively, reduced
by translation for one of the two systems studied, in the evaluated setting.
(4)~The complete harness, audit protocol, prompts, and Wikidata response cache
for reproduction, released anonymously during review.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Multilingual relation extraction and KG population.}
Supervised multilingual relation extraction is anchored by
RED\textsuperscript{FM}~\cite{huguetcabot2023redfm}, which contributes both a
filtered multilingual dataset (human-revised in 7 languages; auto-annotated
SREDFM in 18) and mREBEL, an end-to-end multilingual triple extractor. Of the
eight languages studied here, mREBEL's training coverage includes only Hindi,
a fact we exploit in Section~\ref{sec:secondary} as a controlled
in-coverage anchor against seven transfer languages. Earlier monolingual
end-to-end extraction~\cite{huguetcabot2021rebel} frames the task but does not
separate relation identification from entity grounding in its scoring, which
is precisely the distinction under test here.

\textbf{Fact--text resources for Indic languages.}
XAlign~\cite{abhishek2022xalign} aligns Wikidata facts with sentences in eight
Indic languages for the \emph{fact-to-text generation}
direction, and includes a manually annotated test set. We use this resource in
the reversed direction (sentences as input, aligned facts as extraction gold),
a construction that is ours, not the original authors', and whose
validity we therefore treat as a claim requiring evidence rather than an
assumption (Section~\ref{sec:instrument}). The reliability of reusing
generation-oriented gold for extraction evaluation is itself one of our
pre-registered secondary questions.

\textbf{Translate-test versus direct multilingual inference.}
A recurring finding in multilingual NLP is that translating inputs to English
before inference can outperform direct multilingual processing: language
models are ``unable to leverage their full multilingual potential when
prompted in non-English languages''~\cite{etxaniz2024think}. That
literature, however, evaluates end-to-end task scores; it does not ask
\emph{which component} of a structured task translation repairs. Our
gap-recovery design imports the translate-test comparison into KG population
and decomposes it. The translation arm uses
IndicTrans2~\cite{gala2023indictrans2}, which covers all 22 scheduled Indian
languages; the pre-registered design also carries reference-free quality
estimation with CometKiwi~\cite{rei2022cometkiwi} for a sharpening check
relating residual \textsc{pivot}-arm error to MT quality, not run in this
study (Section~\ref{sec:secondary}).

\textbf{Tokenization and resource proxies.}
Prior work shows the same text can cost up to fifteen times more tokens in
some languages than in English, a disparity that persists in intentionally
multilingual tokenizers~\cite{petrov2023tokenizers}. We carry tokenizer
fertility and pretraining resource share as
exploratory correlates only (Section~\ref{sec:secondary}); neither enters the
primary verdict.

\section{The Instrument: Benchmark and Its Validation}\label{sec:instrument}

\subsection{From Alignment Resource to Extraction Benchmark}
The manually annotated XAlign test split provides, per language,
sentence-level alignments between native-language Wikipedia text and Wikidata
facts rendered as English labels (subject, predicate, object, plus
qualifiers). Two transformations produce an extraction benchmark from it.

\emph{Reversal.} We treat the sentence as input and the aligned facts as the
set of triples an extractor should recover. Because the original annotation
asks ``which of these facts does the sentence express?'', the reversed gold is
by construction a set of facts genuinely asserted by the text (the property
extraction gold requires), while inheriting XAlign's human verification.

\emph{Re-grounding.} XAlign stores facts as English label strings; scoring
against Wikidata requires canonical identifiers. We re-ground without LLM
participation, deterministically in the reproducible sense: given the archived
Wikidata response cache and the released predicate mapping table, the
procedure yields the same benchmark exactly. (i)~Predicate labels map to
property PIDs on an exact (case-folded) label match against Wikidata's
property registry; when the search returns exactly one non-exact candidate,
that lone candidate is taken, flagged for post-hoc review; and for the small
closed set of predicate labels whose English labels have drifted on Wikidata
since XAlign's snapshot, a manually verified override table (five entries,
released with per-predicate match types) supplies the mapping; (ii)~each
subject label is resolved by retrieving candidate
QIDs by name and scoring every candidate by how many of the item's own gold
facts its live Wikidata claims corroborate (the item's facts thereby
disambiguate their own subject); (iii)~object labels are never resolved by free
search: an entity object's QID is accepted only if it appears among the
resolved subject's claim values for that property with a matching English
label, while date and quantity objects are normalized to a literal form and
accepted only if that literal matches one of the subject's claim values for
the property, then retained as literals rather than QIDs. An item enters the
benchmark only if \emph{every} fact resolves. An unresolved subject, an
unresolved predicate, or an unmatched object or literal each drop the whole
item (strict retention), so the benchmark never carries silently missing
gold into recall denominators. Membership in Wikidata is thereby part of the
task definition: the benchmark measures grounding of entities the knowledge
base contains, and NIL detection (recognizing entities Wikidata lacks)
is out of scope by construction.

Table~\ref{tab:gold} reports per-language retention, ranging from
85.5\% (Kannada) to 92.5\% (Hindi); full per-language drop-reason counts are
released with the harness. We expect this loss to be dominated by
drift between the 2020--2021 dumps XAlign was built from and today's Wikidata
rather than by resolution failures intrinsic to the procedure itself, a
pattern the drop-classification audit below confirms; strict retention trades
size for auditability against that expected cost. Retention necessarily
varies by language; because the primary contrast is item-paired within each
language (Section~\ref{sec:test}), differential retention shifts the
composition of \emph{cross-language} comparisons (a caveat those analyses
carry explicitly), but cannot confound the within-language
\textsc{direct}-versus-\textsc{pivot} contrast.

\subsection{Component Metrics and Three-Way Prediction Classification}
Every run is scored with relation-F1 (property identifiers only), entity-F1
(subject and object QIDs only), and full-triple F1, each in a strict
(Wikidata-ID) and relaxed (surface-form) variant. Separating the components is
what allows the hypothesis to be tested at all: a triple wrong only in its
object QID counts against entity grounding, not relation understanding.

Because gold reuse can understate model precision (a model may extract a
true fact the aligned gold simply does not contain), every predicted triple
is classified three ways: (i)~in gold; (ii)~absent from gold but verifiable as
a live Wikidata statement (gold incompleteness, credited in adjusted
precision); (iii)~absent from Wikidata entirely (a hallucination candidate,
analyzed taxonomically in Section~\ref{sec:secondary}). Class~(ii)
verification uses the same cached Wikidata access layer as re-grounding, with
literal-matching rules frozen with the prompts before any scoring.

\subsection{Instrument Validation}
Two independent checks estimate how far the adapted gold can be trusted.
First, a native speaker audited one full language subset (Tamil; 656 source
items) against written guidelines released with the benchmark, recording two
judgments per item: whether each gold fact is genuinely asserted by the
sentence (gold validity), and which sentence-asserted facts are absent from
the gold, each cross-checked against Wikidata (gold incompleteness, the
miss rate that claim C2 tests); the audit protocol treats the auditor as a
measurement device, with the miss-rate threshold (5\%) fixed in the
pre-registration. Second, the
automated class-(ii) rate provides a per-language, scalable estimate of gold
incompleteness for all eight languages. The pre-registration commits us to
publishing whichever way these measurements fall: a miss rate at or above the
threshold, together with a positive automated class-(ii) rate in at least
seven of the eight languages, supports the claim that generation-oriented gold
understates extraction precision; a miss rate below the threshold supports the
pre-registered inverse claim that such reuse is reliable.

The automated estimate came back decisively below threshold: across all 34
(system, arm, language) cells, the share of not-in-gold predictions verifiable
as live Wikidata statements never reaches 5\% (maximum 4.8\%, in one cell;
median 1.3\%; pooled 389 of 30{,}171, 1.3\%). The frozen rule keys the
reliability determination itself to the native-speaker audit's miss rate,
reported next: below 5\%, the inverse claim is licensed; strict
re-grounding yields a benchmark whose precision estimates are reliable, and
what the models predict beyond gold is overwhelmingly not knowledge the gold
merely failed to record. The automated estimate is consistent with, and
supportive of, that outcome.
A drop-classification audit of the retention step points the same way: in a
pre-registered 50-item sample of dropped source items (classified before any
scoring), all 50 are consistent with knowledge-base drift, and exactly one
item in the full 660-drop pool exhibits the procedural signature the
classification was designed to catch (an unparsed unit quantity). A
native-speaker audit of the same language is scheduled prior to submission;
its miss rate, evaluated against the same pre-registered 5\% threshold, is
reported here alongside the automated bound above once complete.

\subsection{Pre-registration}
All decision thresholds, statistical procedures, exclusion rules, and the
outcome-to-claim mapping used in Section~\ref{sec:test} were frozen in a
version-controlled pre-registration committed before any model output was
scored; the scoring harness refuses to run unless it can verify the frozen
commit in its own history. The anonymized repository (including the
frozen pre-registration file, prompts, audit protocol, the archived Wikidata
response cache with its query window, and both the pre- and post-amendment
scored reports) accompanies this submission as supplementary material.

\section{The Test: Direct vs.\ Translate-then-Extract}\label{sec:test}

\subsection{Arms and Systems}
Each system runs \textsc{direct} (original language in,
extract-then-canonicalize prompting) and \textsc{pivot}
(IndicTrans2~\cite{gala2023indictrans2} translation to English, then the
identical English pipeline) over the same low-resource items (these two
arms are item-paired) plus the \textsc{english} control slice, which by
construction contains different sentences (Section~\ref{sec:test},
pairing note).
Systems: Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, both 4-bit (Q4\_K\_M) GGUF
checkpoints served locally with seeded greedy-temperature settings, zero-shot;
exact checkpoints, server build, and decoding parameters are in the released
configuration. One mechanical artifact surfaced at first scoring: Llama-3.1
echoes the canonicalization template's header line before answering, which the
frozen parser's format-echo rule covered at the extraction stage but not the
canonicalization stage; the completion of that rule (content-neutral, defined
by the template string itself, provably inert for the non-echoing system) was
recorded as a dated amendment before re-parsing from the frozen raw archives,
and both pre- and post-amendment scores are released.

Two properties of the canonicalization interface hold identically across arms
and matter for interpretation. First, extraction is open-vocabulary, but
relation canonicalization is closed-set: the canonicalization prompt carries
the inventory of properties attested in the adapted gold, so systems
\emph{select} rather than generate property identifiers; absolute relation-F1
is therefore not open-inventory performance
(Section~\ref{sec:implications}). Second, entity canonicalization is
retrieval-mediated: a mention is grounded by choosing among at most eight
candidates returned by Wikidata's search API (queried in the source
language with an English top-up in \textsc{direct}, and in English in
\textsc{pivot} and \textsc{english}), with the instruction prompts
themselves held constant in English across all arms and languages; only the
passage varies. Retrieval is thus a component of measured entity grounding,
not a controlled-away nuisance: a native-script mention can fail at retrieval
before the model ever chooses. The direction of this asymmetry was presumed
conservative for the hypothesis at design time (had the \textsc{pivot} arm
enjoyed the stronger English retrieval path, a finding of entity
\emph{non-repair} could not have been an artifact of retrieval disadvantage),
while a both-components-repair outcome would have inherited it as a
caveat. The decomposition below voids this presumption for one of the two
systems. We therefore decompose entity errors into retrieval misses versus
selection errors from the archived candidate sets.

The decomposition inverts the presumed direction of the retrieval asymmetry
for Qwen3-8B, the system driving the paper's usable relation-repair
contrasts. Measured subject-candidate recall@8 is \emph{higher} in
\textsc{direct} than in \textsc{pivot} for seven of eight languages (Hindi
$.612$ vs.\ $.518$; Marathi $.547$ vs.\ $.427$; Malayalam $.426$ vs.\ $.296$;
Tamil $.249$ vs.\ $.176$; Kannada is the exception at parity, $.215$ vs.\
$.217$): the native-language query with English top-up retrieves gold
entities that English-side search on translated mentions loses. Llama-3.1-8B
does not replicate this direction. Its \textsc{pivot} recall@8 exceeds
\textsc{direct} in seven of eight languages (e.g., Kannada $.209$ vs.\
$.154$, Hindi $.451$ vs.\ $.423$; Marathi is the exception, $.409$ vs.\
$.418$), so the retrieval mechanism identified here is evidenced for one
system and does not generalize across both by construction. Selection, by
contrast, is high wherever retrieval succeeds in both systems, though not
uniformly near-ceiling: given the gold entity among the candidates, Qwen3-8B
selects it in 92.9--99.5\% of calls per cell, and Llama-3.1-8B in 76.2--94.7\%.
Entity grounding in this setting is therefore retrieval-bound rather than
selection-bound for the system whose behavior licenses the paper's
mechanistic claim, and the \textsc{english} control is itself
retrieval-limited (subject recall@8 $= .549$, Qwen3-8B). Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval}
plots the Qwen3-8B \textsc{direct}-versus-\textsc{pivot} recall@8 values by
language against this control.

\subsection{Gap Recovery Ratio}
For metric $m \in \{\text{relation-F1}, \text{entity-F1}\}$ and each
(language, system) cell,
\[
\mathrm{GRR}_m \;=\; \frac{m_{\textsc{pivot}} - m_{\textsc{direct}}}
                          {m_{\textsc{english}} - m_{\textsc{direct}}},
\]
reported unclipped and clipped to $[0,1]$. Cells whose baseline gap
$(m_{\textsc{english}} - m_{\textsc{direct}})$ is under 8 F1 points are
excluded from verdicts as underpowered (logged and reported); if a metric
retains fewer than five usable languages, its verdict is declared underpowered
and only CIs are reported. Uncertainty comes from a hierarchical bootstrap
(10{,}000 resamples, 95\% CIs): item-paired for \textsc{pivot}-vs-\textsc{direct},
independent resampling for the unpaired \textsc{english} term. Because the
\textsc{english} ceiling uses XAlign's English slice (same domain and
register, different sentences),
the design additionally pre-registers a robustness probe that machine-translates
a sample of English gold items \emph{into} each study language and scores the
\textsc{direct} arm on this synthetic-parallel text against the same gold, giving a
fully paired denominator check (with the translationese caveat stated); prepared but
not run for this draft (Section~\ref{sec:implications}).

\subsection{Decision Table and Verdict}
The pre-registered table maps repair patterns to claims. In brief: relation
repair requires $\mathrm{GRR} \geq 0.75$ with the residual
(\textsc{english}$-$\textsc{pivot}) CI including 0; non-repair is
$\mathrm{GRR} < 0.35$ or an improvement CI including 0; the intermediate band
($0.35 \leq \mathrm{GRR} < 0.75$ with the improvement CI excluding 0) is
scored as partial repair. The hypothesis is confirmed only when relation-F1
repairs while entity-F1 either does not repair or repairs only partially, and
the asymmetry $\mathrm{GRR}_{\text{rel}} - \mathrm{GRR}_{\text{ent}} \geq 0.30$
with a CI excluding 0. Cross-language consistency uses an exact one-sided
sign test in the hypothesis' pre-specified direction (all-but-at-most-one of
the usable languages, exact binomial $p<0.05$; sidedness fixed by a dated,
pre-scoring amendment recorded in the frozen file's log). The primary family
(2 metrics $\times$ 2 systems = 4 tests) is Holm-Bonferroni corrected; system
disagreement on a verdict row resolves to the weaker outcome. Both falsification branches carry their own
pre-registered claims: repair of \emph{both} components would evidence an
English-bound knowledge interface in current open LLMs; relation-F1 itself
failing to repair redirects the explanation toward entity-coverage structure,
tested by popularity-quartile stratification over the retained items'
popularity distribution (dropped items are unscorable, so the quartile claim
is scoped to what the benchmark retains). Any other outcome is reported as
mixed: the continuous GRR profile is published without forcing a headline
verdict, led instead by the gold-reliability finding
(Section~\ref{sec:instrument}).

\subsection{Results}

\paragraph{Usable cells.} The 8-point gap rule excludes more of the grid than
anticipated, for two informative reasons. On entity-F1, the \textsc{english}
ceiling is itself near the floor (Qwen3 $.098$, Llama-3.1 $.143$). Entity
grounding is retrieval-bound even in English, so no language shows a
measurable entity gap for Qwen3 (0 usable; Llama-3.1: 4, below the 5-language
bar). On relation-F1, Llama-3.1's \textsc{direct} scores sit above, at, or
within the 8-point exclusion band of its own English ceiling in every
language (e.g., Bengali $.609$ vs.\ $.515$ above; Kannada $.449$ vs.\ $.515$
within-band), leaving no language with a measurable gap to repair (0 usable).
Both metrics for Llama-3.1
and entity-F1 for Qwen3 therefore fall to the pre-registered escape hatch:
CIs published, no verdict. The one metric--system pair with a testable repair
question is Qwen3 relation-F1, with five usable languages
(Table~\ref{tab:grr}).

under any monotone reading and count it toward no claim it would strengthen.
Cross-language consistency holds, family-uncorrected: all five usable
languages move in the hypothesized direction (exact one-sided $p = .031$).
Under the frozen four-test Holm family this single runnable test
is not family-significant on the conservative reading that unrunnable tests
still occupy the family ($0.03125 \times 4 = 0.125$); on the reading that the
family comprises the tests that could run, it is significant. We adopt the
conservative reading for every claim in this paper and report the other in
this sentence only.

\paragraph{Verdict.} With entity-F1 underpowered on both systems, the
confirmation criterion cannot be evaluated, and neither falsification branch
triggers ($\mathrm{GRR}_{\text{rel}}$ is not below $0.35$ anywhere usable;
both-repair cannot be assessed). The pre-registered outcome is therefore the
\emph{mixed} branch: the continuous profile above is published without a
forced headline verdict, and the paper leads with the gold-reliability
finding (Section~\ref{sec:instrument}). Descriptively (outside the verdict
machinery), the direction of the dissociation is uniform: \textsc{pivot}
improves relation-F1 in 8/8 languages on Qwen3 (7/8 on Llama-3.1) while
\emph{reducing} entity-F1 in 7/8 (Qwen3, e.g.\ Hindi $.216 \to .112$), and
the retrieval decomposition above locates the entity-side damage in the
translated query's weaker retrieval, not in model selection. The
synthetic-parallel denominator probe is scheduled against the released
harness; its absence bounds only the \textsc{english}-ceiling pairing caveat,
which the within-language contrasts do not inherit.

\section{Secondary Analyses}\label{sec:secondary}
% C3 gate resolved to drop (mREBEL is Hindi-only); C5 fragment and the
% QE-correlation sharpening check not run (omitted, not partial); C6
% hallucination-candidate structure reported descriptively below.

\paragraph{Popularity stratification (pre-registered for the B3 branch;
reported descriptively).} Entity-occurrence error rates in the \textsc{pivot}
arm are flat across sitelink-popularity quartiles of the retained inventory:
bottom-vs-top ratio $1.03$ (Qwen3; $.706$ vs.\ $.688$) and $0.89$
(Llama-3.1), far from the $1.5$ criterion. Failures do not concentrate on
obscure entities (famous ones fail almost as often), which is exactly
what the retrieval-bound account predicts and an entity-coverage account
would not.

\paragraph{Sensitivity and composition checks (pre-registered).} Excluding
every item touching the two audit-flagged predicate mappings
(P112, P118) shifts no usable GRR by more than $0.05$ (largest shift:
Marathi, $1.334 \to 1.293$). The keep-rate-vs-GRR
association across the five usable cells is $\rho = 0.60$ (n$=5$,
descriptive only, stated with the retention-differential caveat). GRR CI
half-widths run $0.12$--$0.16$ (Llama-3.1 cells) and $0.22$--$0.54$ (Qwen3
relation cells, wide where GRR is large); per-cell resample counts after the
excluded-draw rule are released with the report.

\paragraph{Supervised-baseline inversion test (C3).} The pre-registered scope
gate recorded a drop: mREBEL, the supervised baseline, has training coverage
that includes only Hindi of the eight study languages
(Section~\ref{sec:related}), so an inversion test against it would be
uninformative for seven of the eight comparisons, a decision recorded at
the gate, before any such run.

\paragraph{Exploratory items not executed.} Two further pre-registered
exploratory items were not run in this study and are omitted rather than
partially reported: the C5 fragment on repair-pattern stability across model
scale, and the QE-correlation sharpening check relating CometKiwi estimates
to residual \textsc{pivot}-arm error.

\paragraph{Hallucination-candidate structure (C6).} Pooled across all 34
(system, arm, language) cells, 30{,}171 predicted triples were absent from
gold; of these, 389 (1.3\%) were verified as true live-Wikidata statements
(Section~\ref{sec:instrument}), and the remainder are hallucination
candidates. Their taxonomic structure is released with the error tables; this
is a descriptive dimension of the error analysis, not a claim.

\section{Implications and Limitations}\label{sec:implications}
% For practitioners (translate first?) and benchmark builders (gold-reuse reliability).
% Limitations: single-family breadth, gold reuse caveats, audit on one language.

For practitioners, the measured profile yields a component-level rule rather
than a blanket one, in this setting. If the quantity of interest is
\emph{which relations} a low-resource text asserts, translating first is
worth it: repair is directionally uniform across usable languages
(family-uncorrected), and in one, the point estimate exceeds the English
ceiling. If the quantity of interest is \emph{which entities} are involved,
translation is, for the system whose retrieval pattern we measured, the
wrong lever (it damages the retrieval path that grounding bottlenecks on),
and effort is better spent on the retrieval interface itself
(native-label indexing, cross-lingual entity search), since selection is
high wherever the right candidate is retrieved, if not uniformly
near-ceiling (92.9--99.5\% for the system whose retrieval pattern we
measured, 76.2--94.7\% for the other). For
benchmark builders, the automated reliability estimate, pending audit
confirmation, points toward a practical recipe: generation-oriented
alignment gold can plausibly be reused for extraction evaluation if it is
strictly re-grounded against the live KB first, at the measured cost of a
$\sim$11\% drop rate that is overwhelmingly KB drift, and with precision
estimates that our automated incompleteness bound suggests are not
materially understated.

\emph{Limitations.} The study covers open LLMs of a single size class under
4-bit quantization; conclusions about scaling rest only on the labeled
exploratory fragment. The gold inherits XAlign's person-biography domain and
its 2020--2021 snapshot, mitigated but not eliminated by strict retention and
live-Wikidata cross-checking. The native-speaker audit covers one of the eight
languages; the other seven rely on the automated incompleteness estimate. The
\textsc{pivot} arm uses one MT engine; the QE correlation bounds, but does not
remove, MT-engine specificity. Relation canonicalization is scored as
selection over the gold's closed property inventory, identical across arms
and languages; open-inventory canonicalization is a harder task, so absolute
relation-F1 does not transfer to open-world settings, though the closed set
leaves the arm and language \emph{contrasts} untouched. The Llama-3.1
template-echo artifact encountered during canonicalization
(Section~\ref{sec:test}) shows that format-compliance parsing is itself an
instrument risk for cross-model comparison; it was handled with a dated,
pre-reparse amendment, and both the pre- and post-amendment scores are
released. The synthetic-parallel denominator probe described in
Section~\ref{sec:test} is prepared but was not run in time for this draft;
until it runs, the \textsc{english}-ceiling pairing caveat rests on the
within-language pairing argument alone. Finally, each cell reflects a single
seeded decoding run rather than repeated sampling, so the results carry no
estimate of decoding variance.
